Galaxy clusters

Confirming ACT clusters and measuring how far away they are

Galaxy clusters are the largest gravitationally bound objects in the Universe, and how many there are, at each mass and redshift, is a sensitive test of cosmology. The Atacama Cosmology Telescope finds them through the thermal Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect: hot gas in a cluster scatters CMB photons to higher energies, leaving a small shadow in the microwave sky. That shadow tells you a cluster is there, but not how far away it is. For that you need optical and infrared images.

The trick is the red sequence. The massive elliptical galaxies at the heart of a cluster stopped forming stars billions of years ago, and they all share nearly the same colour. As the cluster gets more distant, redshift drags their spectral features (above all the 4000 Å break) through the filters, so the colour of the red sequence works as a ruler for redshift. I calibrate that ruler on clusters with spectroscopic redshifts and use it to confirm ACT clusters in Legacy Survey DR11 photometry.

A shadow in the CMB

This is a real patch of microwave sky, about 12° × 7°, seen by ACT at 150 GHz. The blotches, a few hundred microkelvin and around a degree across, are the CMB itself: sound waves in the early Universe, frozen in 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Clusters are far smaller. The boxed one is RCS2 J2327, which you can try for yourself below. Zoom in and it is a cold spot a couple of arcminutes across, deeper at 98 GHz than at 150 GHz, just as the SZ spectrum predicts. The hot spot beside it is a point source, bright at both frequencies.

Find the redshift

Each dot is a galaxy within θ500 of an ACT cluster, roughly the cluster's radius. Drag the redshift slider, or the lower plot, and the shaded band shows where the red sequence would sit at that redshift. When the band lines up with the cluster, the number of galaxies inside it jumps far above what an equal patch of empty sky gives. Can you find each cluster's redshift before you reveal the answer?

    Top: z-band magnitude against colour, from Legacy Survey DR11 g, r, z and WISE W1. The band is my calibrated red-sequence model, ±1σ, from 3 magnitudes brighter to 2 fainter than the characteristic magnitude m*. “Auto” picks the colour that straddles the 4000 Å break at the trial redshift. Bottom: the richness λ(z) from my pipeline, a background-subtracted, radially weighted count of red-sequence galaxies using all three colours. It fills in as you explore. With the empty patch showing, the curve is the same estimator run on that patch instead, drawn on the cluster's axes: it hovers around zero, because there is nothing there.

    Two clusters, one line of sight

    Sometimes the sky lines up two clusters behind one another, and ACT-CL J1137.7−0313 is a clear case: λ(z) has two peaks. To handle this I built a Bayesian model in which every galaxy is a Poisson draw from a cluster's red sequence, its other members or the background, and nested sampling weighs up the evidence for zero, one or two clusters. Force it to fit one cluster and it picks the bigger peak; let it fit two and the evidence leaps.
