Early dark energy

Emulators, profile likelihoods and the Hubble tension

The expansion rate of the Universe today, H0, comes out about 8% higher when measured directly with supernovae than when inferred from the cosmic microwave background assuming ΛCDM. One proposed fix is early dark energy (EDE): a scalar field that sits frozen in its potential, briefly makes up a few per cent of the energy in the Universe around matter–radiation equality, then rolls down and decays away. That brief kick shrinks the sound horizon, and the CMB then asks for a larger H0 to compensate.

Testing EDE against data like ACT DR6 means running a Boltzmann code such as CLASS millions of times, so we replace it with neural-network emulators that predict the spectra in milliseconds. My project asks how good those emulators need to be. EDE makes that hard: the interesting signal sits at small fEDE, and there the parameters that set the shape of the EDE feature stop mattering at all.

A toy universe

To build intuition I made a five-parameter toy model with the same pathologies. A and h play the ΛCDM parameters, f is the EDE fraction, and xc and θ set where the EDE feature sits and its phase. The points are mock data, with noise that grows towards high x like instrument noise at high multipoles. Try fitting them by hand.

The lower panel is the EDE feature on its own. Set f to zero and the xc and θ sliders do nothing at all: the data cannot tell you where a feature is if it isn't there. “Best fit at this f” holds f fixed and lets an optimiser in your browser find the rest. Notice how h rises and A falls as you raise f, much as H0 does in the real model.

The profile likelihood

Those dead sliders are a problem for the usual Bayesian analysis. A marginal posterior for f integrates over everything else, so it rewards regions of parameter space that are merely large, not just regions that fit well. Near f = 0 every value of xc and θ fits equally well, so that corner carries the whole prior volume of those parameters, and the posterior piles up there even when the data prefer EDE. These volume effects depend on the priors you happened to choose, and they are exactly why EDE constraints from MCMC have been so contested.

The profile likelihood sidesteps them. Fix f, let everything else find its best fit, and record how much worse that fit is than the overall best, Δχ². Optimising instead of integrating means a parameter that has stopped mattering costs nothing and gains nothing, so the curve reflects only how well each f can fit the data, whatever the priors. In the toy, f = 0 sits at Δχ² ≈ 7, while everything from about 0.02 to 0.3 lies within Δχ² ≈ 2 of the best fit.

The gold dot is your fit from above, at its Δχ² above the best fit. It can sit on the curve but never below it; if it does, you've beaten my optimiser. The bumps come from xc and θ hopping between solutions as f changes. Every conclusion about EDE rests on the height of this curve near f = 0, which is why the next sections ask how much an emulator can move it.

When emulator error matters

Re-evaluate the same points with CLASS and χ² moves by up to 1.8, and it moves most at small fEDE: exactly where the profile decides how strongly fEDE = 0 is disfavoured.

Lower panel: χ²CLASS − χ²emulator at each profile point, almost all of it from the ACT DR6 CMB likelihood. The CLASS curve re-scores the emulator's best fits rather than re-optimising, so each point is an upper bound on what a full CLASS profile would give there.

How accurate is accurate enough?

So how small does an emulator's error need to be? Whiten everything by the data covariance, so each band power's error is measured in units of its own σ, and call the emulator's RMS whitened error e. Over N = 387 ACT and Planck band powers its total error is s = √N e, and the χ² error it causes is

Δχ² = s² − 2 r̂·δ̂  ⇒  |Δχ² − s²| ≤ 2 (√λ + z) s,

where δ̂ is the emulator error, r̂ the data residual and λ the profile's Δχ² at that point. For small errors the s² term is negligible, so the χ² error grows linearly with RMSE, with a slope set by how far the point sits from the best fit. That turns a tolerance on the profile into a requirement on the emulator.

Each point is one emulator at the chosen profile point: χ²emulator − χ²CLASS for ACT DR6 + Planck at the same parameters, against its RMS error in units of σ per band power. The shaded wedge is the prediction with z = 1: far from the best fit it holds every emulator, and near it about two thirds, as a 1σ band should. Blues are the same network trained on 25k, 50k and 100k spectra, ten seeds each; grey points were trained on a doubled prior box; gold points are named versions. Hover for details. Move towards the best fit (λ → 0) and the wedge closes, because errors matter far less where the profile is flat.

An AI agent on the night shift

Part of the emulator design was done by an autonomous coding agent. Over two overnight runs it proposed a change to the network or its training, trained it under a fixed time budget, scored it on held-out spectra, and kept the change only if the error went down. Most ideas failed, which is what research looks like. Press play to watch it work.
